# Refreshers: Unit 2 and Literature Review

By now, you should have a general sense of what writing a literature review entails: it's a process of evaluating, (re)considering, selecting, analyzing (breaking down and examining the source's various components), and synthesizing (integrated and organizing your insights in a way that leads to new connections and insights).

# Literature Review Refresher

Still shaky on what a literature review is and how to do it? Recently figured out that you need to redo your preliminary research because your research hypotheses aren't narrow enough yet? Here are more resources to help you!

> ###### 🛑 Stop: Read!
> This [self-guided tutorial](https://iu.libguides.com/literaturereview){:target="_blank"} by Indiana University LibGuides defines a literature review &mdash; including its writing-in-the-disciplines (WID) dimensions &mdash; and demonstrates how to search, critically read, analyze, and synthesize sources in assembling your literature review.
{: .block-tip }

By now, your literature review should consist of a few key authors who are studying the specific research area you're investigating. These sources form what Pacheco-Vega calls "the backbone of the literature review." 

You may also have sources that provide context for one specific aspect of your research questions &mdash; in short, providing a contextual framework for your study. Don't confuse these courses for "background sources," which is often used to refer to objective statements, statistics, and other facts. You might have learned in high school that background sources are important for fact-based reports or position papers, which ask you to regurgitate facts or defend a preestablished position on a polarizing topic. **Neither of these approaches belong in advanced qualitative research.** 

Instead, try approaching the literature review through the lens of a contextual framework, or a rationale and foundation for your study, by keeping the following question in mind while you draft: 

- What is the context you need to create for your readers in each section of your literature review, so they understand what your research is about, why it's necessary or significant, and how it contributes to the scholarship in your field of study?

This question will help you figure out which insights to include from your selected sources, as well as how to organize your sources in and across each section of your literature review to tell a logical story about the research you're conducting.

> ###### 🛑 Stop: Read!
> Dr. Raul Pacheco-Vega's blog post on ["How to Undertake a Literature Review"](../assets/pdfs/raul_pacheco-vega_how_to_undertake_a_literature_review.pdf){:target="_blank"} further describes qualitative research in the context of doing a literature review.
{: .block-tip }

Pacheco-Vega describes the following steps in his "walkthrough" of writing a literature review:

- He immediately divides his research area into two thematic categories based on the work of the anchor authors he's going to use
- He uses citation tracing &mdash; checking all the sources you've selected to see how many of your chosen authors are citing each other &mdash; to figure out whether or not he's reached conceptual saturation
- He limits himself to 1-2 ideas floating around in the literature and reads the authors working on one or both of those ideas until conceptual saturation is achieved
- He uses a mindmap sketch to visualize relationships between articles and concepts, but using the Literature Review Matrix handout or Conceptual Synthesis Dump Excel spreadsheet would be just as effective.

To prepare yourself for drafting the literature review, replicate these steps for your own Qualitative Research Projects!
 
> ###### Conceptual Saturation and Citation Tracing
> A more precise definition of conceptual saturation is when you see the same citations repeated across the research you're doing (the Works Cited of your various sources can show you this at a glance). This is a strong sign that your area of research and approach has gotten narrow and specific enough. # Writing the ENG 201 Lit Review

Once you've filled out the Literature Review Matrix handout or Conceptual Synthesis Dump Excel spreadsheet, you'll need to read through your sources and select the 6 that are most relevant and useful for your qualitative research project's focus. You probably have some sources in your list that initially seemed like they could work (based on the title, keywords, some portion of the argument, etc.) &mdash; but if now it seems like they no longer serve the needs of your project, exclude them from your literature review.

**The most important thing to remember is that a literature review is not just a series of summaries about articles related to your topic.** Don't spend all your time summarizing and doing nothing else! Your readers aren't looking for a recap of articles they could read on their own; they're trying to learn something from *you*, about *your research* specifically.

Considering the following questions can help you prioritize synthesis over summary:

1. How does the article address some part of your research hypotheses, i.e. why even mention this article in your paper? (This usually appears in the first sentence of the paragraph)
2. What distinct argument is each article making? (How you introduce the article afterwards, often a few sentences)
3. What are the findings in each article? (The end of the paragraph, often 2-4 sentences)

As you read and reread these articles, take extensive notes in your Conceptual Synthesis Dump Excel spreadsheet and/or Literature Review Matrix sheet. Referring to these notes and your annotations in the articles themselves will help you identify and specifically name commonalities between articles. The names of these commonalities will be the subheadings for each of the two subsections in your Literature Review.
