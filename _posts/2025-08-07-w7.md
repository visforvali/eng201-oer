# Using Feedback

From now until the end of the semester, you're tasked with revising your paper using feedback you get from your teammates after workshop, from me in #🙋classroom Q&A and #team activities, and one-on-one coffee chats (which aren't required in async sections but are highly recommended). 

The best way to approach revision and writing is to change your writing habits. As Cassuto (2024) says, "Writing is thinking, not just a record of your thoughts. Only by actually writing do you figure out what you want to say, and how you should say it." One of the hardest parts of research for student writers is letting go of ideas like "what a good paper sounds like" or "what a college professor wants to read" or "I need to make a point and prove it instead of exploring and thinking on the page." 

Ballenger (2018) says that good questions have the power to open doors to discovery, even with things you might consider uninteresting. Research is a messy, imperfect process, with lots of missteps, unexpected turns, and conclusions that may fruitfully diverge from your initial research questions.

Aside from being bound by the assignment guidelines &mdash; i.e. if you don't do what the assignment asks you to do in the spirit that you're asked to do it, you won't receive credit &mdash; you should approach research writing with an open mind and a willingness to let your thinking change as your research progresses and to decouple from ideas, claims, or drafts that no longer work.

At this initial stage, revising isn't about editing for lower-order concerns (LOCs), like word choice, flow, rhythm, etc. It's about higher-order concerns (HOCs): clear articulation of your ideas, how they logically progress, etc.

If you struggle to recognize where you're making assumptions, try entering those assumptions into Google Scholar to see what comes up, or ask yourself, "Would my professor or a common-sense person challenge this, or need more to understand or accept it?" If you're not sure if the assumption would be challenged or interrogated, then don't present it as a generalization or universal fact. 

Here's an example: Just because your experience in online classes isn't good or you've taken courses with instructors who aren't trained in online teaching, it doesn't mean online courses or effective digital pedagogy are new to education or generally ineffective. Repeated scholarship searches using terms that are properly narrow and complex would easily uncover this fact, while simplistic, single searches wouldn't. Thus, presenting such a statement as fact would reveal to your readers that your research was not appropriately conducted.

# What Do I Do With Feedback?

You can (and should!) use feedback to plan revision. Eli Review suggests that using feedback is a four-step process: reflecting, where you make sense of the feedback you received; prioritizing, where you decide what feedback matters most; strategizing, transforming that into a specific plan for revision; and applying, or making changes based on that plan.

Eli Review's post on [Using Feedback to Plan Revision](https://learn.elireview.com/students/using-feedback-to-plan-revision/){:target="_blank} walks you through the first three steps of this process of considering and integrating feedback; their post [Revise Your Writing](https://learn.elireview.com/students/revise-your-writing/){:target="_blank} discusses the fourth step.

As you come up with revision plans of your own, review the peer feedback you received as well as the [General Feedback Sheet](https://visforvali.github.io/eng201-oer/pages/feedback/){:target="_blank} and ask yourself: 

- What is the single most significant issue that you need to work on in order to meet the objectives for this particular draft and unit?
- What would improvement of that specific issue look like right now?
- Where would you need to make changes in order to see that improvement most clearly?
- From a writing-in-the-disciplines (WID) perspective, how might you articulate these changes in writing to optimize your revision?

# What Are Primary and Secondary Research? 

This is a semester-long assignment, so sometimes you'll need to look ahead to later units of drafting to make sure you're thinking about the bigger picture throughout the drafting process. **Secondary research** is library research, while **primary research** involves gathering data from original sources &mdash; through unanalyzed vlogs, in this class. 

As we move into Unit 2, you might find it useful to skim parts of Caulfield's [Web Literacy for Student Fact-Checkers](../assets/pdfs/caulfield_web_literacy_for_student_fact_checkers_2017.pdf){:target="_blank}, as the literature review hinges on your ability to evaluate the viability of sources for research.
