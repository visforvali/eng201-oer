# What's Grounded Theory and Why Should I Care?

Grounded theory is the name for an approach to doing research and building theory "from the ground up" &mdash; meaning "from the data." Grounded theory research begins with data in the form of secondary research (library research) and primary research (fieldwork). The grounded theory researcher takes an active, interpretive role in approaching their secondary research with few to no predetermined thoughts and no preexisting literature review. In our class, you're this grounded theory researcher; your experience influences the research questions that are asked and the research hypotheses you come up with. 

You'll be collecting data and conducting analysis by categorizing evidence and figuring out the relationships between these categories &mdash; "from the ground up," starting this week with casual, cursory preliminary research meant to familiarize you with the contemporary discourse around current problems, concerns, or tensions that are impacting *members* (not clients or the general public!) of your field. For instance, a student pursuing a career in mental health care might focus a set of research hypotheses around how GenAI use by clinically anxious patients has impacted how *psychotherapists* conduct a therapy session, *not* how the patients are affected by using it.

> ###### Looking Ahead!
> Because your experience is a valuable part of grounded theory, reflecting on your process of doing and writing research is important! This is why, whenever you engage in #team workshops, you'll also write short process reflections intended to help you become aware of your metacognitive ("thinking about thinking") research processes and how they influence your approach to grounded theory and qualitative research. 
{:.block-warning}

Engaging in grounded theory doesn't mean you're developing a grand theory at the level of established philosophers. Grounded theory research is about looking for patterns in the data that you collect &mdash; starting this week with the preliminary research you'll be doing.

# How Do I Prepare for Grounded Theory Research?

As you might be able to guess, grounded theory approaches require a lot of loosely guided searching, previewing, and skimming &mdash; reading strategies outlined by Carillo &mdash; where you revise your search strings in each new search based on increasingly specific keywords and creative synonyms and substitutions based on the results of previous searches. 

Research starts with keywords and phrases. One way to start identifying important keywords for your discipline is to look up the professional associations in your career field (such as flagship conference organizations) and preview their websites to get a sense of issues that have been historically significant and issues or problems that have surfaced more recently. Position statements on these websites will also offer language that might be useful for assembling search strings.

For instance, a student writing about healthcare workers in the mental healthcare field might visit the American Psychological Association, where just clicking the "Topics" tab suggests that a top issue in the field right now is the dangers of GenAI on adolescents' psychological development and health. Then, using Google Scholar, the writer would try entering combinations of those keywords along with search strings that guide them towards increasingly specific potential research questions directed at the impacts on clinical practitioners.

If you struggle with developing your research hypotheses and reach out to me for guidance, I'll ask to review your preliminary research process so I can better help you; thus, I recommend writing down all the significant discipline-specific language, keywords, and phrases you come across, as well as all the combinations and synonyms you try in search strings, in a document we can reference together as needed this semester.

# Forming Research Questions

You won't be drafting your research questions until next week, but through the process of grounded theory research, you'll begin organically developing them this week. Your questions will arise out of what you learn from your preliminary research. They'll evolve, mutate, and become increasingly specific the more research and drafting you do throughout the semester. Thus, this process entails simultaneously reading and writing &mdash; going back and forth between skimming articles and writing keywords, ideas, notes, and memos for yourself.

Barroga & Matanguihan (2022) describe the development of research questions as:

> "A process based on knowledge of current trends, cutting-edge studies, and technological advances in the research field. Excellent research questions are focused and require a comprehensive literature search and in-depth understanding of the problem being investigated. Initially, research questions may be written as descriptive questions which could be developed into inferential questions. These questions must be specific and concise to provide a clear foundation for developing hypotheses. Hypotheses are more formal predictions about the research outcomes."

> ###### 🛑 Stop: Watch!
> Read this page and watch the embedded video ["Picking Your Topic is Research!"](https://www.lib.ncsu.edu/videos/picking-your-topic-is-research){:target="_blank"} produced by NC State University Libraries, which walks you through the process of choosing a topic using research, and using research to test the viability of your chosen topic.
{:.block-tip}

Researchers tend to ask discipline-related kinds of questions, leading to a variety of scholarly inquiries within one research topic. Palmer (2020) offers the example of "renewable resources" and poses multiple discipline-specific questions:

- *Business, Accounting, Economics*: "Which renewable resources offer economically feasible solutions to energy issues?"
- *History*: "When did humans switch from the use of renewable resources to nonrenewable resources?"
- *Environmental Studies*: "How can algae be developed at a pace and in the quantities needed to be a viable main renewable resource?"
- *Geography*: "Which U.S. states are best suited to being key providers of renewable natural resources?"

Like many scholars, you'll need to combine those kinds of questions with the particular asks of the qualitative research project for ENG 201: e.g., that you're investigating the impact of a contemporary issue in your discipline *on members of your field* as opposed to clients or the general public. 

You'll learn more about developing research questions and hypotheses next week, when we talk about drafting discipline narratives, research questions, and problem statements!

# Strategies for Active Reading and Annotation 

You'll engage with the below strategies more deeply when we get to the literature review, but they may come in handy as you embark on your preliminary research. 

Dr. Raul Pacheco-Vega blogs quite a bit about academic writing that work at the graduate and undergraduate levels. Among these strategies, he explains ["How to Map Out Debates in the Scholarly Literature"](../assets/pdfs/raul_pacheco-vega_how_to_map_debates_in_scholarly_literature.pdf){:target="_blank"}. This is one way of positioning yourself and your inquiry in a body of existing literature. Of particular interest to our class is the "Empirical/Case Based Approach: Which case better illustrates a particular element." For this research project, you'll want to avoid approaching your project from a binary position (e.g., for/against).

Here are some of the steps Pacheco-Vega outlines for mapping debates that might be useful for your research process:

1. Identify the core element of discussion
2. Identify the type of debate you are finding in the literature: Are authors positioned along a continuum? Does the scholarly work you are finding support polarized positions (yes/no, pro/against)? Has the debate evolved over time? Is it theoretical, methodological?
3. Trace the different arguments and authors

He also recommends mapping specific citations. For this, you might want to glance at the Works Cited of articles that seem especially useful, and write down those authors and titles for later previewing or skimming.

> ###### 🛑 Stop: Free-Write!
> To prepare for the Research Introduction, try conducting 3 rounds of preliminary research on your own right now by following the directions below.
{:.block-tip}

First, make a list of keywords that apply to your discipline or career and that describe a problem or issue that you think is plaguing your field. You might generate this list of keywords by exploring professional organizations within your discipline (as recommended above) and/or by listing keywords that have come up frequently in your major-related classes. Create a search string that's as specific as possible: e.g., "bioinformatics" is a more meaningful search than "computer science"; "neonatal intensive care unit nurse" is stronger than "nursing"; and "racial inequity in salary" is stronger than "salary difference."

Plug that search string into Google Scholar. Using previewing strategies, go through the first ~50 results provided by Google Scholar, including article titles and abridged summaries. Write down keywords, phrases, and conceptual vocabulary that feel useful.

> ###### Important!
> Make sure to focus on the *issue faced by members of your discipline* (not the clients or publics they serve!) in one part of your search string; in the other part of the search string, include *relevant subdisciplines or even specific occupation titles*. Keep modifying and adding to your search string as you progress.
{:.block-warning}

Next, use the notes you just took to refine all of the terms in your search string. The goal is to get to a line of inquiry so specific, you won't have to spend hundreds of pages exploring every corner of it once you start drafting. **It might feel counterintuitive to you, but the more specific your research hypotheses are, the fewer pages you'll need to write to thoroughly, satisfactorily explore them.**

Next, conduct a new Google Scholar search using your updated search string. Using previewing strategies, review ~25 results &mdash; article titles, abridged summaries &mdash; and this time, open those ~25 articles and skim the abstracts as well, using only the "abstract" part of Pacheco-Vega's AIC technique for now.

> ###### Practice: Carillo + Pacheco-Vega
> As you read the articles your preliminary research uncovers, practice combining Pacheco-Vega's AIC technique with the reading strategies Carillo described in the book chapter you read last week, especially previewing, skimming, reading and evaluating, and reading like a writer. 
{:.block-tip}

As before, but with more (and different!) information, write down phrases, words, and keywords that feel useful. Your expanded list might have made some of your previous keywords less useful or redundant; take a moment to cross those out. What themes are emerging for you now? What controversies, debates, and problems appear to plague members of your discipline (e.g. funding, staffing issues, overwork and burnout, lack of emotional support, low salaries, frequent demoralization, identity bias, inequality, etc.)? 

Take a moment to jot your observations down.

Using your new keywords, refine all of the terms in your search string again, and repeat your search with the new, updated search string. Using previewing, skimming, reading and evaluating, and AIC strategies, review ~25 results &mdash; article titles, abridged summaries &mdash; and open the articles and skim the abstracts, introductions, and conclusions using Pacheco-Vega's AIC technique. As before, but with more (and different!) information, write down phrases, words, and keywords that feel useful. If any items on your previous list now feel redundant, take a moment to cross those out. 

> ###### Important!
> Don't worry if your search string becomes so specific that the number of results shrinks from 100s to 10s. When you conduct research for the literature review, you'll be looking for sources that *don't* exactly match your research questions. Again, it might seem counterintuitive to you, but the more specific your research hypotheses, the *less* you'll end up having to write and rewrite later on.
{:.block-warning}

Keep doing the above until you're down to fewer than 500 results. 

Finally, make a list of 5-10 of the articles that feel closest to inspiring your research hypotheses &mdash; and skim these articles in search of the authors' research hypotheses. 

Now, from everything you've just read, what seems like a specific problem or issue impacting members of your field right now? What *didn't* you see in your preliminary research that might serve as a gap, a place where you can contribute to the conversation? 

## Demo: Preliminary Research!

In case it helps to reference an example, here's one of [my old blog posts](https://vyshalimanivannan.com/conducting-online-preliminary-research/){:target="_blank"} where I demonstrate my own messy process of conducting preliminary research online. Since I'm a disabled professor, in this post I model one way to narrow down my general interest in a project about being a disabled academic and professor. I really want to ask why academia is ableist, and why disabled instructors can't be treated better, but these aren't research questions that I can reasonably, thoroughly address in even 20 pages. Pay attention to how I experiment with my keywords in particular, as creatively coming up with synonyms and substitutions is an integral part of the preliminary research process.

In the linked example, I might come away with the following, using Dolmage's *Academic Ableism*, Brown & Leigh's articles, and a few others to explain some background and form a set of research questions:

> Disabled writing instructors, especially those with invisible disabilities, are under-represented in academia due to the risks of disclosure (the possibility of being fired or discriminated against) and a lack of architectural accessibility. Fibromyalgic writing instructors suffer from incurable chronic pain that isn't always visible, and writing itself is a painful activity even with ergonomic setups. My research questions right now are: How do fibromyalgic instructors negotiate access in the writing classroom through disclosure and accessible pedagogy? How do they use accessible pedagogies to accommodate themselves and their students? What do their strategies tell us about ableism in academia, especially writing studies?

If I pursued this project, I would eventually offer tentative answers to those questions after close-reading the necessary articles, and those answers will become my research hypotheses.

It's important to use preliminary research on Google Scholar to make it sound like you are coming up with your research questions from existing scholarship, and not out of thin air, just personal experience, or a random Google search.
