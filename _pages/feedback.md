## What Is General Feedback? 💯

After the first draft in each unit, i.e. Draft 1, Draft 3, and Draft 5, I'll update this page with general feedback &mdash; comments about issues that span 90%+ of the papers in the class &mdash; to help you identify, locate, and address issues in your teammates' and your own drafts during subsequent asynchronous peer workshops. (I'll also participate in these peer workshops, so you'll have the opportunity to verify if you're accurately correlating issues on the General Feedback Sheet to your draft.)

By practicing figuring out &mdash; from a finite set of options &mdash; which numbered revision tasks apply to your draft, you'll get better at recognizing similar issues on your own, making you better able to independently revise your writing across the curriculum. 

Be sure to also review my weekly Q&A responses to you and your classmates in #🙋classroom discussion and #team activities. More information about assessment of high-stakes projects is on the [💯Alt-Grading System](/eng201-oer/pages/alt-grading){:target="_blank"} page. 

## Be Accountable for the Work You Did or Didn't Do

By now, you're expected to be familiar with the requirements of this course and to be accountable for the work you are or aren't doing in this course. Refer to the High-Stakes Writing: Qualitative Research Project section of the [Assignments]([✍️ Assignments · 🗓️ ENG 201](https://visforvali.github.io/eng201-oer/pages/assignments/)) page (as well as the [Alt-Grading System]([💯 Alt-Grading System · 🗓️ ENG 201](https://visforvali.github.io/eng201-oer/pages/alt-grading/)) page) every time you sit down to draft and revise your project. It might also be helpful to refer to revisit all the modules (including all the linked resources in them) for the unit of drafting you're working on.

# A Note on Scoring

> ###### Important!
> Drafts of high-stakes writing are scored on the basis of completion in a manner that demonstrates attention to and progress towards fulfilling the assignment guidelines. Drafts that actively work towards the project's stated purpose, engage with the material, and are unafraid of "getting things wrong" on the way to figuring things out are accepted and will be marked "R" for "Reviewed." If I can't accept a draft for credit for substantive reasons, I'll mark it "N" for "No Credit." **"N" scores are given if you don't submit a draft by the end of the grace period, if your submission doesn't demonstrate a connection to the assignment or to our course content thus far, or if you did not use instructor feedback.** Receiving an "R" for one draft doesn't mean you don't need to revise or guarantee that you'll receive credit for the next draft. In fact, **revision between drafts is mandatory.** 
{: .block-warning }

Drafts are scored in this fashion to allow you to experiment with how you implement feedback without fear of penalty.

You might be used to writing courses where you're explicitly told what to do, as though writing and revision are processes that belong to someone else &mdash; processes of making corrections that another person notices and tells you how to fix. In reality, revision has to start and end with you! But since writing is also as an activity of thought that's socially, collaboratively improved, my role as your writing instructor is to provide you with guidance and just enough direction for you to be able to apply your own thinking and figure out what to do with each item on your own after attempting to locate it in your own work on your own. This allows you to think through how to say something &mdash; what research or other work needs to be done in order to say something &mdash; and then decide how you need to put it into action, based on feedback during peer workshops, my responses to your weekly Q&A posts, and any feedback I provide on other low-stakes writing assignments.

## How to Use This List ✅

After peer workshops, you should combine the peer feedback you received with the numbered revision tasks below. I encourage you to collaboratively work through this General Feedback Sheet with your teammates (asynchronously in your #team channel or synchronously in 🔊☕Cafe if you and your teammates can coordinate a time).

To help you prioritize your revision tasks, when I post scores to Classes, I include personalized feedback for you in the form of the three numbered revision tasks on this General Feedback page that must be addressed by the next draft. **However, you will likely need to revise more than just those three items.**

> ###### Note
> To view scores on Classes, navigate to Classes/Grades. To view your top three revision tasks, navigate to Classes/Assignments and click the "Feedback: Unread" link in the "Evaluation Status" column. 
{: .block-tip }

When working through the whole list of General Feedback, I recommend you do the following: 

- Read the first numbered revision task, then read your draft with that revision task in mind to see if you can catch the issue on your own
- If you locate it, mark it and make notes to yourself regarding how to address it
- Repeat this process with the next numbered revision task, and so on and so forth!

In my experience &mdash; apart from blatantly ignoring the directions &mdash; the revision tasks that appear in the General Feedback for this course are fairly common issues in writing-in-the-disciplines (WID) classes, so don't feel like you're alone or don't know what you're doing if you find your draft contains most of the tasks below.

## Types of Feedback

For ease of revision, feedback will be divided into 2 categories: higher-order concerns and lower-order concerns:

**Higher-Order Concerns (HOCs)** include the most important, macro-level material in your essay: the thesis, claim, or controlling idea; specific connections between your interest in the field and the scholarly discourse in the field; development of your research questions and hypothesis (i.e., "what am I investigating, how do I know what I know, what do I expect to find out?"); primary and secondary research; your analysis of each piece of evidence; and so on.

**Lower-Order Concerns (LOCs)** include the less important (but not unimportant!), micro-level material in your essay, primarily grammar and mechanics: spelling, fragments, run-ons, punctuation, sentence rhythm variation, word choice, and so on.


## Unit 1 Draft 1 General Feedback

### HOCs

1. To receive credit, **your research questions (RQs) must follow the Qualitative Research Project assignment guidelines, and your overall project must reflect weekly modules and weekly Q&A feedback from me**. As per the assignment guidelines state, your project must focus on 2-3 narrow aspects of a contemporary (within the last 5 years), specific problem that impacts *members of your discipline* (i.e., the professionals working in your career field). You should have discovered and narrowed this problem during your 3-4 week process of using grounded theory to conduct preliminary research.
2. As the Unit 1 modules, assignment guidelines, and my Q&A responses and feedback on your RQs have taught you, your RQs should consist of a set of at least 3 *related* questions, each aimed at unpacking the particular problem you're exploring, explaining, and reflecting on with increasing specificity. Through exposition and specific research questions, your research introduction should identify and briefly describe this problem, consider how it manifests in your discipline/career and affects professionals working in your discipline/career, as well as what its overarching ramifications are for your field in general.
3. Your question and hypothesis will evolve and change as we keep doing research. That said, at every stage of drafting, your audience should get the sense that you are informed by relevant scholarship regarding the impacts of the contemporary problem on members of your discipline under exploration--so they can trust you aren't omitting anything obvious and that your RQs are emerging from the literature instead of solely from you/in isolation from preexisting discourse. Through preliminary research, your writing should simulate disciplinary authority by demonstrating disciplinary features and familiarity with the existing scholarly literature and contemporary discourse of the field. It should be clear to readers that grounded theory approach to preliminary research played a major role in the development of your RQs and identification of the exigence for the particular problem you're exploring.
4. Your research *questions* are *plural*: You should have a set of at least 3, consisting of at least a "what/what kind(s)" question (identifying the phenomenon and discipline) and "why" and "how" questions (describing the problem/phenomenon in more detail). (See my example preliminary research tutorial in the Unit 1 modules for a refresher.)
5. Reduce the variables present in your RQs to reduce the amount of research and writing you'll have to do to thoroughly, appropriately address them. Arriving at specific research questions means you must narrow every variable present in your set of research questions that you can. For instance, you should be examining a subset of a demographic that is as specific as can be, which means considering several factors of identity and profession: race, gender, sexuality, age, nationality, geographic location, specific job sector or type of company/corporation (e.g., investment banking or tax advising; the for-profit or nonprofit sector; or writers in the horror or the science-fiction genre). Your research questions should never engage in generalization and speculation about large demographics of people (i.e., don't make claims about "people" and treat a group of people like "writers" or "teachers" like a monolith) and/or their motivations or reasons for behaving a certain way, which will always be wildly varied.
6. Avoid the following in your research questions: RQs that focus on the clients or people served by your discipline and not the professionals working in your discipline (i.e., follow assignment guidelines); RQs that can be simply answered with yes/no, a statistic or statement of fact, or a commonsense guess that your reader could guess before reading your whole paper; RQs that focus on problem-solving or strategies to address a situation, as your goal is to describe, explore, and interpret a phenomenon, not fix it (as you can't in a paper of this length); RQs that are inarguable because they revolve around a large population's motives or reasons that are diverse and that you can't possibly know (e.g., questions like "Why do people want to major in my field?," "Why do publishers reject certain literary themes?," or "Why are consumers influenced by social media trends?" are inarguable; even if these are "why" questions they speculate about motives rather than help you describe a phenomenon).
7. Your discipline narrative must be relevant to the RQs you're proposing for your project. In other words, the discipline narrative should feel necessary to understanding your interest and motivations for exploring *those particular RQs about your field*, not your broader motivations for pursuing your career. Part of your task as a writer-researcher is to create those connections for your audience so they don't have to guess or impose their own preconceived notions onto your research project.
8. As you revise for Unit 1 Draft 2, use your preliminary research and material from Modules 2-5 to turn your RQs into RHs by posing tentative answers to your questions. These tentative answers should be based on the final set of papers you skimmed in your preliminary research process.
9. A research hypothesis is specific enough when you can't make it any more specific--when every variable in it has been made as specific as it can be. If you find by the literature review that it's too specific later, you can begin to broaden your scope, as it's much less work to begin with extremely narrow and slowly broaden your focus than start broad and have to do multiple rewrites to make your RQs specific enough that they can be thoroughly explored in a paper of this length. As you engage in new rounds of research (whether preliminary research or focused research for the literature review), it's *always* better to use keywords that return too few sources and then expand your research questions and alter your keywords than it is to have to refashion a general research hypothesis into something more specific. Being hyper-specific with your word choices when entering them into scholarly database search engines allows for more concentrated and precise results in the articles and information that search yields. Don't worry about being too specific! This is rarely possible, as discussed in our weekly modules and Q&A, and there are plenty of workarounds if few results come when you search scholarly databases (e.g., don't search for your exact RQs; or focus on one specific type of professional or another specific variable but draw on contemporary literature on a related discipline facing a similar problem to help you think through your own project).

### LOCs

Unit 1 consists of a lot of idea generation, prewriting, and initial articulation of a discipline narrative and research framework. At this point, you shouldn't worry about issues of grammar, mechanics, formatting, correct and consistent citation, and other LOCs. 


## Requirements for Feedback Coffee Chats

You're welcome to schedule a coffee chat to discuss your project with me (caveat: see my thread in #🍕lounge regarding my surgical scheduling this semester). Because this is an async class, I expect you to email me all your practice/"on your own" work in advance, including all documentation related to your research thus far (preliminary or otherwise), including your keywords, repeated search strings, and selected and vetoed sources, and all practice writing exercises from the unit modules. I expect you to come to our coffee chat prepared to "teach back" material to me so I can better pinpoint the source of your confusions. To do this, I will ask you to explain the Qualitative Research Project guidelines to me and try to identify what you haven't yet addressed in them and explain specific, useful examples from the unit modules. If you can't produce the necessary documentation, can't "teach back" relevant material, and/or come unprepared to review the material in real-time, I will cancel our chat and ask you to reschedule for a time when you're better prepared.
